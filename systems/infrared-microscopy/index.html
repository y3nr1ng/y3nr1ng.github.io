Infrared laser-scanning microscopy

Infrared light, scanning optics, and instrument control for chemical imaging.

An infrared chemical image records how absorption changes across a specimen. Different molecular vibrations contribute at different wavelengths, so the measurement gives us chemical contrast alongside spatial structure. The challenge is to collect those measurements over a large area while preserving both image detail and reliable spectra.

In the Chemical Imaging and Structures Laboratory (Bhargava Lab) at UIUC, we develop infrared laser-scanning microscopes for this work. The 2023 IR-LSM paper brings together custom infrared optics, confocal detection, and synchronized acquisition. My contribution to that publication was programming microscope subroutines; my broader work on these instruments includes control software, firmware, electrical integration, and hands-on operation.

Choosing the chemical measurements

The quantum cascade laser is tuned to one infrared band at a time. A scan forms an image at that band; repeating the scan at selected bands adds chemical information at the same positions. We can acquire a small set of bands for a particular measurement or sample more of the spectrum when additional chemical information is needed. Each added band also adds acquisition time.

The recorded signal needs a reference: the published workflow measures a blank substrate before imaging to account for laser power and non-uniformity. This connects a detector reading to an absorption measurement that can be compared across positions and wavelengths.

Scanning a focused beam across the sample

The instrument sweeps a focused beam with galvanometer mirrors. Its objectives, tube lens, and scan lens were designed together for the broad mid-infrared range, where common visible-microscope glasses are unsuitable. A confocal pinhole rejects stray reflections before the signal reaches the detector.

The fast scan axis records in both directions, reducing time spent returning the beam between lines. That also creates a coordination problem: detector samples from the forward and reverse sweeps must land at the correct image positions. The published system tracks the scanner’s position signal to align the lines, while the data-acquisition hardware synchronizes scanning, laser triggers, and detector readout.

Sharpness is only part of the measurement. The paper evaluates spatial contrast with test targets and modulation transfer functions (MTF), alongside noise and spectral agreement with FT-IR measurements. Together, these checks examine whether the instrument preserves spatial detail and usable chemical information.

From one field of view to a whole section

The adult zebrafish image below shows why coverage matters: the same section contains brain, gills, muscle, liver, intestine, and ovary. Selected infrared bands produce differences in contrast across these structures without adding stains to the section being measured. These are false colors assigned to the measured bands, rather than the specimen’s visible appearance.

A whole section spans many scan fields. The stage moves between overlapping tiles, and the images are blended into a mosaic. The acquisition therefore needs to coordinate position, focus, wavelength selection, and frame collection across the sample, rather than simply record one image repeatedly.

Averaging without mixing measurements

Averaging repeated frames reduces noise, but only while they describe the same measurement. In the 2023 system, software keeps a circular history of recent frames for averaging. When the monitored stage, laser, or other equipment changes state, that history is cleared so frames from different conditions are not mixed. The software then collects the image channels as the laser steps through the requested bands.

This is one example of how instrument state becomes part of image formation. A measurement depends on the optics and detector, but also on the software knowing which samples belong together.

My work on the instruments

My primary work is control software and firmware, with additional contributions to electrical design. I also help build, align, integrate, and maintain the microscopes. These are team-built instruments: the 2023 paper credits Kevin Yeh with the original control software and microscope build, and credits me and Manu Mehta with microscope subroutines.

My ongoing software work includes ScopeEngine, which organizes acquisition workflows, and ciMicroscope, a desktop application for operating the microscope. The software page describes that framework and its device interfaces. The published instrument above provides context for the measurements this broader software work supports.
