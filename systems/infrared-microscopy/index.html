Infrared laser-scanning microscopy

Infrared laser-scanning microscopy for chemical imaging across whole tissue sections.

Infrared absorption provides chemical contrast without adding stains or fluorescent labels. Different molecular vibrations contribute at different wavelengths, so images collected across those bands reveal chemical differences alongside tissue structure.

In the Bhargava Lab at UIUC, we develop infrared laser-scanning microscopes to collect these measurements over large areas. The instrument has to preserve spatial detail and reliable spectra while covering a whole section. My work connects the control software and firmware to that physical measurement.

Scanning a focused infrared beam

The optical layout below is from our team’s 2023 IR-LSM paper. It combines a tunable infrared source, scanning mirrors, custom refractive optics, and confocal detection. The optics must maintain a focused spot across both the scan field and the range of infrared bands being measured.

The published design records both scan directions and aligns detector samples using scanner position feedback. It also measures a blank substrate to reference laser power and non-uniformity. These details make image formation a coordinated measurement, with position, wavelength, and detector readings kept in agreement.

From individual scans to a chemical image

The adult zebrafish section below contains many organs within one image. It was assembled from overlapping scan fields, with selected infrared absorption bands assigned to false-color channels. The colors show differences in the measured chemical contrast, without staining the section being measured.

Collecting a section like this means repeating acquisitions across positions and bands, then retaining the information needed to interpret the resulting channels. A small set of bands can provide selected chemical contrast; sampling more of the spectrum adds chemical information and acquisition time.

The published figures document the team’s earlier instrument. Kevin Yeh led its microscope build and original control software; the 2023 paper credits me and Manu Mehta with microscope subroutines.

My work on the instruments

My primary contribution is control software and firmware. I also work on electrical design and help build, align, integrate, and maintain the instruments. The ScopeEngine article explains the software engine I wrote, including acquisition jobs, device integration, data handling, and the WIP survey of other acquisition software.
