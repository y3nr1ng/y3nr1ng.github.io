Single-molecule localization microscopy

Reconstructing microtubules and nuclear pore proteins, and moving the analysis onto a cluster.

The first question was whether we could reconstruct microtubules across multiple cells in three dimensions. The next was how long we needed to collect blinking molecules before the reconstruction contained enough detail. Moving to nuclear pore proteins added another constraint: the cell could move while those observations were accumulating.

Our 2019 Communications Biology paper explores these questions using light-sheet illumination and single-molecule localization microscopy (SMLM). I designed the data-analysis program. For me, a particularly engaging part was moving the processing onto the lab’s cluster and keeping it running, including work with Dask in its early days.

Reconstructing microtubules across cells

We began with α-tubulin, a component of microtubules, labeled in mouse MC3T3-E1 cells. Microtubules form an extended network through a cell, providing structures we could examine across successive depths and compare with conventional light-sheet images.

A fluorescent molecule appears as a blurred spot in an individual camera image. When a sparse set of labeled molecules blinks, we can estimate their positions and combine many observations into a reconstruction. Repeatedly scanning the volume collects more of those positions throughout the cells.

Collecting enough observations

A shorter recording provides fewer localizations; a longer recording can fill in structures, but costs acquisition time and is exposed to drift and bleaching. We needed a way to examine how much additional detail those observations actually provided.

The graph in Fig. 2h uses Fourier ring correlation (FRC) to estimate lateral resolution. In broad terms, FRC examines agreement between independent reconstructions at different spatial scales. Here, the estimated resolution improved from about 107 nm after 75 volumes to about 75 nm after 250 volumes. The improvement eventually levels off, helping identify when further acquisition provides diminishing returns. This measures detail supported by the reconstructed data; it is distinct from the microscope’s optical resolution or the uncertainty of one fitted molecule position.

The paper also reconstructs microtubules in primary hippocampal neurons from rat pups, using astigmatism to estimate molecular positions within each optical section.

Keeping the fluorescence background low

Dense labeling helps us collect localizations, but fluorescence from outside the plane can obscure individual spots. A scanned Bessel beam confines excitation to a thin region. The sample moves through that region while the illumination and detection planes remain fixed, collecting a volume without requiring the two planes to track one another across the specimen.

From fixed pores to living cells

Nuclear pore complexes regulate transport across the nuclear envelope. In fixed HeLa cells, POM121 labeling let the team reconstruct pore structures; aligning and combining selected pores gave an estimated diameter of about 100 nm (Fig. 4).

Living cells change the tradeoff. Accumulating more observations can improve the sampling of a structure, while motion during that interval can smear it. For live POM121 imaging in NIH 3T3 cells, the paper examines a single plane and reconstructs 40-second windows from frames recorded at 20 ms exposure each.

The movie below moves to three-dimensional mapping of Nup153 in live HeLa cells. The fluorescence view provides context for the localizations. These observations follow the protein distribution over time; they do not continuously resolve individual pore structures during rapid nanoscale changes.

Moving the analysis onto the cluster

The recording arrives as a sequence of volumes, but drift correction needs to follow the same depth over time. The analysis reorganizes the TIFF stacks into a time series for each layer, then uses ThunderSTORM with customized macros for localization. Results from the layers are collected into one localization list for reconstruction and geometric analysis.

The published workflow used Lustre storage and a four-node TORQUE cluster. My work on ThunderSTORM cluster scripts and Dask on TORQUE was part of moving SMLM analysis onto shared computing infrastructure and keeping processing running. Acquiring enough molecular observations and computing their reconstruction are different constraints; this software work addressed how we handled the recordings once they had been collected.

The small in-lab cluster also supported later light-sheet work, with batched processing across multiple nodes, dataset storage, and bookkeeping.

Bi-Chang Chen and Chieh-Han Lu constructed the microscope for the 2019 study; the instrument control software was licensed from HHMI/Janelia.
