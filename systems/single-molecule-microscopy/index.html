Single-molecule localization microscopy

From blinking fluorescent molecules to three-dimensional images and distributed analysis.

Fluorescent molecules appear as blurred spots in a microscope image. When only a sparse set emits at once, we can estimate their positions individually. Combining many such observations builds a more detailed view of the labeled structures. This is single-molecule localization microscopy, or SMLM.

Our 2019 Communications Biology paper combines localization with light-sheet illumination to image three-dimensional specimens.

Illuminating one plane

A scanned Bessel beam forms a thin illuminated region, reducing fluorescence from outside the plane being recorded. Repeated acquisitions capture blinking molecules throughout the volume.

Turning recordings into an image

The analysis reorganizes volumetric TIFF recordings into a time series for each layer, corrects drift, and localizes fluorescent spots with ThunderSTORM. Layer results are then combined into one localization list for reconstruction.

Following nuclear pores in a living cell

Nuclear pore complexes regulate transport across the boundary of the nucleus. Here, the protein Nup153 is labeled so that we can follow its distribution through a living cell in three dimensions.

My part in the processing

I designed the data-analysis program for this work. The published workflow uses custom ThunderSTORM macros, Lustre storage, and a four-node TORQUE cluster to process the recordings. Bi-Chang Chen and Chieh-Han Lu constructed the microscope; the instrument control software was licensed from HHMI/Janelia.

My related software includes ThunderSTORM cluster scripts and Dask on TORQUE. These belong to my SMLM processing work, alongside localization processing and three-dimensional geometric analysis.
