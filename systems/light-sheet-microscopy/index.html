Light-sheet microscopy

Imaging three-dimensional specimens with sheets of light, patterned illumination, and optical stimulation.

A light-sheet microscope illuminates a thin plane inside a specimen while a camera looks at that plane from the side. Recording successive planes builds a three-dimensional image. At Academia Sinica, I worked with axial-scan, dual-view, and lattice light-sheet systems. The papers here describe the team’s lattice light-sheet work.

Imaging a volume

The illumination and detection objectives approach the specimen from different directions. In lattice light-sheet microscopy, patterned light forms the thin illuminated region. Moving the specimen through it lets us record a volume, and repeating that acquisition lets us follow changes over time.

Larger specimens and finer detail

Our 2020 Methods paper looks at expanded specimens: biological structures embedded in a gel that physically swells, making small features easier to distinguish. The larger specimen also means more volume to image. The team combined specimen preparation with a tiling lattice light-sheet approach to reduce specimen translation during acquisition.

The 2020 Journal of Physics D paper explores structured illumination. Instead of recording one image per plane, we record several with shifted illumination patterns and reconstruct an image from them. The two-beam interference design uses three pattern phases for two-dimensional structured illumination within the light-sheet microscope. My work included implementing SIM reconstruction algorithms.

Using light to influence living cells

The 2022 optoLLSM paper adds optogenetic stimulation: light activates engineered, light-sensitive proteins in a cell. The instrument positions a stimulation beam while recording three-dimensional fluorescence images. This connects a local intervention to the cell’s response over time.

Building the system and handling its images

For optoLLSM, I helped design and construct the instrument with Wei-Chun Tang, Chieh-Han Lu, and Bi-Chang Chen. The microscope’s control software builds on the SPIM program licensed from HHMI/Janelia. The published imaging experiments and image processing were carried out by Wei-Chun Tang and Bi-Chang Chen.

Across my light-sheet work, I also developed acquisition tools and software for volumetric processing, including fusion of multi-focus, non-gridded datasets. The related uToolbox and DeconvLR repositories cover other parts of my image-processing work.
